    A ratio (likely dimensionless) is measured

Bremsstrahlung spectrum average Cross section for 96Ru(gamma,n)95Ru
    Bremsstrahlung based experiments use photons (with a distribution of energies) created using high energy electrons hitting some substance. The value measured is inherently an integral (considering each photons energy and the relative quantity of it produced). So we do not use this, since we do not know the exact energy of the incident photon.

(( Cross section for 206Pb(gamma,n)205Pb )+( Cross section for 206Pb(gamma,n+p)204Tl ))
    Inherent sum of cross sections. In this case, Gemini tells me this is because the detectors used in the facilities could not detect protons, and hence you cannot separate these two reactions.

Cross section for electric polarity given for 207Pb(gamma,n)206Pb ( / magnetic polarity)
    The photons that were used here were of a specific polarity (polarity effects on cross section). Our experiments consider a single energy, but within that all polarities, so this is not valid. 

Relative data Cross section for 16O(gamma,n)15O
    The values here will likely be ratios, which we don't want. Gemini tells me the detectors might not be sensitive enough to measure actual values, but can measure relative differences (perhaps via anotther detector measuring some effect of this reaction)

[1] Cross section for 116Sn(gamma,n)115Sn, Corrected for energy spread of LCS beam based on the Taylor expansion method; [2] Cross section for 116Sn(gamma,n)115Sn, Corrected for energy spread of LCS beam based on the least square method; [3] Cross section for 116Sn(gamma,n)115Sn, Average cross sections for two methods
    The LCS (Laser Compton Backscattering) beam is quasi-monoenergetic (the distribution has a large spike at one energy, with very low amplitudes at other energies.) Techniques like Taylor approximations are used to approximate the curve, and provide the true "single energy" cross section. Similarly for least squares (a different technique to approximate the same value). And these may be averaged. I've not considered them for simplicity.

Re-read the PINN modeling in the paper. It has several loss terms
1) MSE loss (Standard)
2) Lorentzian - Penalizes non Lorentzian like shapes for the function sigma(energy) (the form is 1/((x- c)^2 + 1)).
    $$\sigma(E_\gamma) = \frac{\sigma_1 E_\gamma^2 \Gamma_1^2}{(E_\gamma^2 - E_1^2)^2 + E_\gamma^2 \Gamma_1^2}$$
    E_1 represents the GDR energy (energy at which the nucleus has the highest chance of absorbing the photon, and entering into resonance). \Gamma_1 represents the width of the function (in MeV) at which the cross section drops to half its maximum value. \sigma_1 represents the peak cross section (y value). Together, these parameterize the Lorentzian curve. Since our PINN works with normalized data, \sigma_1 is 1, whereas \Gamma_1 and E_1 need to be normalized.
3) L_{mono} - Discourages upward slopes beyond the GDR peak. This makes sense since measurement noise might induce an upward slope. However, I wonder whether the Lorentzian doesn't handle this implicitly?
4) Threshold loss - Forces sigma = 0 below the neutron separation (Sn) energy. 

We need information about \Gamma_1, E_1 and Sn to fully specify the loss function. Sn is available from the ame2020 data, E_1 and \Gamma_1 are available from RIPL-3 and the output-target for prediction (sigma) is available through EXFOR.
